Technical Study — No. 042

The Science of Microbial Succession

An in-depth exploration of how humidity, limestone porosity, and ancestral brine dictate the flavor evolution of alpine rinds.

Published: May 12, 2024 By: Pierre Moreau

"The rind of a cave-aged wheel is not merely a protective layer; it is a living ecosystem, a landscape of yeast and bacteria that breathes the history of the cellar."

The Limestone Catalyst

In our Lyon caves, the walls are composed of high-porosity Jurassic limestone. This geological feature acts as a natural humidity regulator, absorbing excess moisture during the damp spring months and releasing it slowly during the dry winters. This constant, gentle respiration is critical for the development of Brevibacterium linens.

Microbial life doesn't just inhabit the cave; it negotiates with it.

As the wheel ages, we observe a predictable succession. First, the yeasts de-acidify the surface, preparing the environment for the salt-tolerant bacteria. By month six, the rind begins to take on its characteristic ochre hue, a visual signal that the enzymes are beginning to break down the proteins within the paste.

The Affinage Protocol

Brushing & Brining

Every forty-eight hours, the wheels are hand-turned and brushed with a secret solution of sea salt and morge. This labor-intensive process ensures even distribution of the surface flora and prevents the paste from drying prematurely.
